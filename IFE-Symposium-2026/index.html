I.Overview

Inertial Fusion Energy (IFE) reactors expose first wall materials like tungsten and steel to high intensity radiation and thermal cycling. Understanding the strain these conditions produce is vital to the construction of future reactors and ultimately power generation facilities.

The damage that matters happens at the scale of individual atoms — a vacancy here, an interstitial there, a lattice plane pushed half a bond out of place. This work asks whether Bragg coherent diffractive imaging (BCDI) can see damage at that scale, and answers it by simulating irradiated tungsten nanograins, computing the diffraction they would produce, and then trying to recover the atoms from nothing but those intensities.

This page is the supporting material for the poster. It shows a curated selection of those reconstructions — the ones that worked, and the ones that did not — because the failures are as informative as the successes.

IV.The archive at a glance

Every reconstruction below comes from the same pipeline: a tungsten nanograin is built and relaxed with molecular dynamics, irradiated in a separate MD simulation, its diffraction is computed at three {110} Bragg peaks, and the solver is then handed only those intensities and asked to produce atoms. The truth is never shown to the solver — it is used afterwards, to score.

There are 513 scored reconstructions with a full figure suite. A run counts as converged when its fit reaches the measurement's own noise floor (χ²/floor < 5), and as failed when it stalls far above it (> 50). Those two thresholds need no knowledge of the truth, which is what makes them usable on real data.

What that table does and does not say

It is a success rate per attempt, not a capability limit. When a 5,000-atom reconstruction works it recovers the atom count exactly and every position to about 0.001 Å — roughly a thousandth of a tungsten bond. And the single best result in the archive is not a small grain at all: 26,067 atoms, recovered exactly. The question this archive answers is not can it, but how often, and why not always.

The 50,000-atom row is honest about work in progress: 79 of those 82 runs sit in the intermediate band, improving but not yet at the noise floor.

The best result in the archive. Twenty-six thousand atoms, and the solver found the count exactly — not approximately, exactly — with a mean position error of 0.0008 Å. VI.When it fails

Four reconstructions that did not get there. None of them crashed, none of them threw an error, and every one of them produced a plausible-looking grain full of atoms. That is exactly why the failure diagnostics matter.

Set the two sheets side by side and the failure is unmistakable. The R-factor drops for about twenty iterations, flattens at 0.23 — fourteen times its own noise floor — and then stays there for the remaining 180 iterations. The atom count never reaches the truth line, ending 118 atoms short. And the teal recall trace on the position-error panel never leaves zero: from the first iteration to the last, not one atom of 9,334 comes within half an ångström of a true site. The final mean position error is 1.42 Å, about half a tungsten bond. The run was not short of time; it was stuck.

The number that hides this

The recall in the strip above is 0.9605, which looks like success. It is measured at a 2.023 Å matching tolerance — but a tungsten bond is 2.7411 Å, so that tolerance is only 74 % of a bond and an atom sitting on the wrong lattice site still counts as found. Re-measured at an honest 0.5 Å, this run's recall is 0.0000. Not one atom of 9,334 is actually where it belongs. How the tolerance got that loose →

The worst run in the archive, and the most instructive. It invented 9,325 atoms — a 38 % overpopulation — and its R-factor of 0.595 is 63× the noise floor. More photons did not help; this is a search failure, not a data problem. And note where it happened: at higher dose than the runs that merely stalled, which is the clearest evidence in the archive that these failures are not caused by lack of signal.

A 0.95 recall on a grain that is 38 % too big

At the 2.023 Å tolerance this run scores recall 0.9501. At 0.5 Å it scores 0.0015. Its median atomic displacement is 1.38 Å and its precision — the fraction of the atoms it produced that correspond to a real one — is 0.6905. Any one of the loose-tolerance numbers, quoted alone, would describe this as a good reconstruction.

VII.The seed lottery

The poster's remedy for a failed reconstruction is to run several in parallel from slightly different initial conditions. Here is the measurement behind that sentence.

Eight reconstructions were run against one 9,334-atom grain. The truth file is identical across all eight, byte for byte, as is the measurement recipe: three {110} peaks at 7.5×109 photons, 200 iterations, the same sampling fraction. Every configuration value matches except one random seed.

That seed does two things in this pipeline. It draws the photon shot noise of the simulated measurement, and it chooses which subset of measured voxels the solver fits at each iteration. So these are not eight reruns over one frozen dataset — they are eight independent repeats of the same experiment on the same crystal, the computational equivalent of carrying one grain back to the beamline eight times. Three converged and five did not.

Three of eight, or 37.5 %. The seed-3 run carries a directory name from an earlier experiment; its config.toml was checked line by line against 10k_s13's and differs only in the seed and in two atom-count bounds derived from it.

Why this is usable rather than merely discouraging

χ²/floor separates these eight runs cleanly — 1.15, 1.16 and 1.53 against 151, 275, 938, 1046 and 1204 — and computing it requires no knowledge of the truth. It is the model's fit to the measurement you actually took, divided by the best fit photon statistics would permit. So on real data you can launch several reconstructions, keep the one with the lowest χ²/floor, and discard the rest without ever knowing the answer. Measured across the archive, that truth-free rule captures 99.3 % of the improvement an all-knowing oracle would achieve.
